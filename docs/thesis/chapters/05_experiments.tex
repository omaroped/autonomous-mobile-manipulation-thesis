\section{Mapping and Navigation Performance}

\subsection{Map Quality After Sensor Correction}
The SLAM mapping session was conducted twice: once with the erroneous $360^{\circ}$
LiDAR configuration and once after the sensor parameters were corrected
(Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}). The resulting occupancy grids were compared
by counting spurious obstacle cells in regions known to be unobstructed in the
Gazebo world geometry. The uncorrected map contained 55 isolated occupied cells
in open free space; the corrected map contained zero such cells. The corrected
map spans 238$\times$358 cells at 0.05\,m/cell, covering the simulated workspace
of approximately $11.9\,\text{m}\times17.9\,\text{m}$, with 97.2\,\% of cells
classified as free. The absence of spurious cells is the primary quality gate for
map acceptance, since a single phantom wall cell in a narrow corridor can render
a planned path infeasible.

\subsection{Navigation Success and Localisation Stability}
The differential-drive navigation stack was validated by commanding the robot to
a sequence of goals distributed around the simulated workspace, including a goal
at the box pickup location. The robot reached all commanded goals without manual
intervention. AMCL particle convergence was verified visually in RViz by confirming
that the scan overlay remained aligned with the map walls throughout the navigation
trajectory, including during in-place rotations at path start and end. Quantitative
position error at goal arrival and localisation covariance data are reserved for
the hardware deployment phase, where real odometry drift will produce non-trivial
errors; in simulation with \texttt{odometry\_source: WORLD}, position error at goal
is determined entirely by Nav2's goal tolerance parameter (0.05\,m linear,
0.1\,rad angular) rather than by odometry accumulation.

\subsection{Reachability as the Binding Constraint}
The dominant finding of the manipulation experiments is that workspace reachability,
rather than planning or perception, governs grasp feasibility for this platform. The
nominal $0.28$\,m specification of the myCobot~280 describes the maximum reach of the
arm extended horizontally; the effective reach for a \emph{top-down} grasp is
substantially shorter, because orienting the gripper vertically requires the wrist to
fold, consuming reach. From the measured link geometry the elbow-to-wrist span is
approximately $0.226$\,m, which is insufficient to keep the wrist above a target
located $\approx 0.24$\,m forward; consequently a purely top-down grasp at that
distance admits no inverse-kinematics solution. Empirical reach maps, generated by
sweeping candidate poses through the \texttt{/compute\_ik} service and recording
success, confirmed this analysis.

\subsection{Orientation-Dependent Reachability}
A second result is that reachability is a function of the full target orientation,
including the redundant wrist roll, and not of position alone. For an identical target
position, one top-down wrist roll over-twisted the wrist at extension and yielded no
solution, whereas a roll rotated by $90^{\circ}$ was solvable across the workspace.
This sensitivity is significant for grasp planning: fixing the tool orientation to a
single value can make the usable workspace appear materially smaller than it is, and
reach must therefore be evaluated over a family of orientations. The interaction of
this short top-down reach with the mobile base's forward overhang and the
forward-mounted camera (Chapter~4) defines a narrow feasible window jointly governed by
arm mounting, base stop distance, and workspace layout---a constraint specific to
mobile manipulation and absent from fixed-base systems.

\subsection{Workspace Reachability Map}
The reachability analysis was quantified by sweeping candidate end-effector poses across
a grid of positions and orientations and recording inverse-kinematics success via the
\texttt{/compute\_ik} service. In a representative $2025$-pose sweep, the top-down
orientation was reachable at far fewer grid points than an angled or side approach
($194$ reachable poses top-down versus $290$ side-grasp), confirming that the top-down
orientation is the binding constraint and that a side or tilted approach materially
enlarges the usable workspace. An independent $750$-pose sweep recorded $25.9\%$ overall
reachability, with the reachable envelope contracting sharply beyond $x\approx0.18$\,m.
These maps were produced by an automated sweep tool and are treated as primary
experimental data, providing the empirical basis for the chosen grasp distance and
orientation.

\subsection{Grasp Execution in Simulation}
Stable grasp execution in Gazebo required addressing rigid-body contact behaviour. A
position-controlled gripper commanded into a rigid surface produces large separating
impulses that, with a comparatively light and unbraked base, destabilise the entire
platform. This was mitigated by (i) terminating the descent clear of supporting
surfaces and registering them as planning-scene collision objects, (ii) constraining
the base during manipulation, and (iii) adopting the established simulation convention
of rigidly attaching the object to the gripper at the grasp instant rather than relying
on simulated friction closure. These measures are characteristic of pick-and-place in
Gazebo and are reported here as part of the methodology rather than as a limitation of
the approach. With the tool-frame calibration of Chapter~4 applied (lateral offset,
tightened orientation tolerance, and closing clearance) and the surface contact model
softened, the simulated manipulator executes a centred, vertical top-down grasp that
lifts, holds, and replaces the target repeatably.

\section{System Integration and Logistics Simulation}

\subsection{End-to-End Navigation--Pick--Place Cycle}
The full integrated task cycle was executed in simulation: the robot navigated
autonomously from its starting position to the pickup table, docked visually to
the box target, grasped and lifted the target object, and backed up from the
table. The cycle was demonstrated successfully, with the grasped object remaining
rigidly attached to the gripper throughout the backup manoeuvre. This constitutes
a functional validation of the orchestration layer and confirms that the Nav2,
perception, MoveIt, and weld-attachment subsystems cooperate correctly under a
single coordinating node.

\subsection{Arm Transport Posture and Inertial Stability During Navigation}
A recurring fault during integration testing was joint oscillation at the arm's
second joint during base navigation. Joint~2 of the myCobot~280 is a revolute
joint with a horizontal rotation axis; in the rest (home) posture the distal arm
links extend upward and forward, creating a significant lever arm about that axis.
Under base acceleration forces this configuration acts as an inverted pendulum:
the arm's inertia excites a low-frequency oscillation at joint~2 that persists
for several seconds after the base stops. Joint~1, which has a vertical rotation
axis, is not excited by forward--backward forces and exhibits no corresponding
oscillation.

The fix adopted is a compact transport posture (\texttt{travel}) in which joint~2
is commanded to $+1.2$\,rad (folding the arm forward and down) before navigation
begins. This reduces the moment arm of the distal link mass about joint~2 and
lowers the overall system centre of mass closer to the base, attenuating inertially
driven oscillations. The transport posture is commanded via MoveIt at the start of
the orchestration sequence, before the Nav2 goal is sent, ensuring that the arm
adopts the compact configuration before any base motion occurs.

\subsection{Grasp-Weld Localisation: Stale Pose Failure and Live TF Fix}
During initial integration testing the grasp-weld service incorrectly placed the
simulated box after the robot had navigated to the table. Investigation revealed
that the service stored the robot's world position once at node startup and used
this value---without updating it---to compute the gripper's world position at grasp
time. After navigation, the base occupies a different position; the stale startup
position produced an incorrect world-frame gripper pose, causing the box to be
placed at an incorrect location relative to the world rather than at the gripper.

The fix replaces the stored startup position with a live TF lookup:
\texttt{tf\_buffer.lookup\_transform(`odom', `gripper\_tcp')} is called at the
moment of grasping. Because \texttt{odometry\_source: WORLD} makes the
\texttt{odom} frame numerically identical to the Gazebo world frame, this lookup
returns the gripper's true world position regardless of where the base has driven.
With this correction, the box is placed at the correct gripper location and moves
with the gripper as the arm lifts and the base reverses.

This failure mode---a node consuming a pose derived from the system state at startup
rather than at the time of use---is a common source of integration faults in mobile
manipulation systems where the base is free to move between node initialisation and
task execution.
